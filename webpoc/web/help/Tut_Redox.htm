Iron corrosion

The diagram shows the corrosion products formed as a function of the amount of iron metal added to water. These steps use the eq-diagr database, which has magnetite, Fe3O4(cr); the KTH course database does not.

  1. In the Chemical system tab, choose eq-diagr database in the list Database (or in the Database menu).
  2. Click Fe in the periodic table and then Fe 2+. Click e− (the electron, in the top row of the periodic table). The selected components are H+, Fe2+ and e−.
  3. Click Search reactions and then Create a diagram.
  4. To show the corrosion products as a function of the iron metal that has reacted, Fe(cr) must be a component instead of Fe2+. Open the Chemical system section of the diagram tab and, in the row of Fe 2+, choose Fe(cr) in the list exchange for…. The components are now H+, e− and the solid component Fe(cr).
  5. In the Diagram section, choose Fe(cr) as the X-axis component.
  6. For corrosion in pure water, nothing is added but iron: the total concentrations of H+ and e− must be zero. In the Concentrations section, set the type of H+ to total conc. with the value 0, and do the same for e-. For Fe(cr) choose total conc. varied from 0 to 0.01 (10 mmol per kg of water).
  7. Click Plot the diagram.

The diagram shows the amounts of the corrosion products (in mol per kg of water) as a function of the iron metal that has reacted (in millimol of Fe(cr) per kg of water). Magnetite is the solid corrosion product, and hydrogen gas is formed:

3 Fe(cr) + 4 H2O → Fe3O4(cr) + 4 H2

Reduction of Cu(II) to Cu(I) by cyanide

In a laboratory exercise the students react Cu2+ and Cd2+ with CN−. The experiment with Cd gives a clear solution, while Cu precipitates.

Illustrate first the Cd experiment with a diagram for [Cd]T = 10 mM and a varied CN− concentration. Assume that the Cd solution had pH 4 at the start, that is, set the total concentration of H+ to 10−4 M (figure). Why is there no precipitate? What would happen if OH− were added instead of CN− (figure)?

With copper(II) a redox reaction takes place:

Cu2+ + CN− + e− → CuCN(s)
CN− → ½ (CN)2(g) + e−

Make a diagram to see what happens, with the components e−, CN− and Cu2+. If you select both Cu2+ and Cu+ as components you can model the solubility, but not the redox reaction. Use the same concentrations as for Cd, and set the total concentration of added electrons to zero, [e−]T = 0, to simulate the redox process. See the step by step instructions.

There is an "ideal" CN− concentration where almost all Cu precipitates. With an excess of ligand, soluble Cu(I) complexes form and the solid dissolves again.
